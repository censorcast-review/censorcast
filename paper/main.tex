\begin{abstract}
Selective prediction often counts accepted cases even when its risk denominator measures unequal exposure. Accurate high-exposure cases can then supply error budget for many inaccurate low-exposure cases. We develop a budget audit of this mismatch, supported by sharp examples, impossibility conditions, and a bounded-exposure envelope. With a fixed predictor and common candidate menu, changing the calibration utility changes the selected rule. In retrospective retail comparisons, exposure-based selection increases demand coverage by 7.64--10.63 percentage points at a cost of 35.18--40.66 case-coverage points; a mobility comparison exchanges 13.34 case points for 5.01 exposure points. These are utility-dependent reallocations, not improvements under both objectives. A stricter threshold design with a separate-window risk check preserves the disagreement at lower coverage. Its bootstrap evidence is approximate. A previously frozen external retail test confirms transfer of a selected contrast; improving the predictor separately increases both coverages. Evaluation should specify acceptance utility, disclose both sides of the exchange, and check risk independently of coverage selection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A selective prediction system must specify both its risk constraint and the value of acceptance. Counting accepted cases gives each case equal value. Counting their exposure, opportunity, or demand mass gives cases different weights. When risk is itself a ratio of accepted loss to accepted weight, the choice of coverage utility becomes part of the learning problem.

Case coverage can increase while covered exposure falls. Equal value per case remains a legitimate objective, but cannot stand in for exposure coverage. The threshold rankings are established; our contribution is to quantify their disagreement. We give nearly maximal joint gaps, a bound under bounded exposure, and conditions excluding reversal. The acceptance unit matters: fixed multi-label prediction with whole-example abstention can reverse the objectives, whereas ordinary binary precision with an unrestricted gate admits a common optimum.

Retail WAPE makes the mechanism concrete. Well-predicted high-volume cases create pooled error slack that pays for many poor low-volume predictions. Our CENSORCAST evaluation suite measures this mechanism; it is not a new architecture. We preserve its original holdout tests and add a non-retail mobility comparison.

We then ask which rule an evaluator would select from a shared five-rule menu. Case and exposure utility can select different rules, including a simple baseline rather than the fitted ratio. A separate audit designs thresholds in one window and screens risk in another before utility selection, returning no choice if every candidate fails. These menu audits are retrospective; the original frozen external test has a narrower confirmatory role.

\section{Related work and scope}
\paragraph{Count-data evaluation.}
\citet{kolassa2016count} explains why absolute-error measures, including weighted MAPE, can be unsuitable for retail count forecasts. Absolute loss elicits a median; when $\Pr(Y=0)\geq1/2$, zero is a median. Our new object is the acceptance budget: a poor relative-error row may still be inexpensive to accept. M5 established strong tabular forecasting baselines \citep{makridakis2022m5}; we use LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm}. The mechanically censored retail experiment preserves complete targets for evaluation and isolates heterogeneous forecast errors. It is one measurement setting, not a premise of the theory. Natural-stockout identification diagnostics are retained in Appendix~\ref{app:natural-diagnostics}.

\paragraph{Ratio metrics and selective prediction.}
Optimization of nondecomposable and ratio-of-linear classification metrics is well established \citep{dinkelbach1967,koyejo2014,narasimhan2015complex,bao2020fractional}. Those works optimize prediction performance under the chosen metric. We fix the predictor and compare acceptance utilities under the same ratio constraint, asking how much case coverage can disagree with covered denominator mass.

\paragraph{Selection and risk control.}
Case coverage and its risk trade-off are foundational to selective classification \citep{elyaniv2010foundations,geifman2017selective}. Conditional-loss thresholding is established in reject-option prediction \citep{franc2023reject}, as are group concerns in selective regression \citep{shah2022selective}. \citet{jones2021} show that selective classification can magnify group disparities. \citet{fisch2022} study calibration within the selected population; our distinct question is which population mass coverage rewards. Learn then Test and conformal risk control address calibration under explicit assumptions \citep{angelopoulos2021ltt,angelopoulos2024crc}. \citet{joshi2026postprocess} maximize agreement with a baseline decision policy under a chance constraint, ranking contexts by the reduction in conditional violation risk from a fallback action. Our action is forecast acceptance, the constraint is signed expected excess $\E[a(e-r\mu)]\leq0$, and the utility can weight either rows or demand. Accurate high-volume cases can therefore subsidize inaccurate low-volume cases within an accepted set. Conditional thresholding remains the common mathematical tool; our analysis concerns this cross-case budget mechanism and its measurable consequences. Calibration is treated separately.

\paragraph{Scope under censoring.}
The mismatch theorem does not require censored observations. Its inputs are the conditional loss and exposure of a fixed predictor. Mechanical censoring lets us retain the complete target to estimate and audit these inputs; natural stockouts generally do not. Observed sales alone cannot certify a cap below one when compatible demand tails are unbounded (Appendix~\ref{app:identification}). This is a limit on identifying the theorem's inputs, not an additional explanation of objective reversal.

\subsection{Calibration is a separate claim}
\label{sec:calibration}
Independent calibration clusters and known bounded cluster losses permit simultaneous finite-family concentration checks; the supplement states a standard Hoeffding construction (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}). Our retail counts lack a justified population bound and share store/calendar shocks. Accordingly, development intervals are descriptive; the held-out comparisons use prespecified item-bootstrap approximations, not that theorem.

\section{Audit design}
\subsection{Data, predictors, and chronology}
Our primary retail menu compares three fixed predictors on the same M5 development partition: a hierarchy-feature L1 forecaster with category scaling, its learned residual correction, and a frozen univariate Chronos-2 configuration. The 1,829 items span all ten stores (18,290 series). A common demand head and one squared-loss error head per predictor use 600,000 training rows on days 1555--1610. Calibration windows A and B cover days 1611--1645 and 1646--1673; evaluation covers days 1800--1913 (2,085,060 rows), called Later in the result tables. Full model and information specifications are in Appendix~\ref{app:accuracy}. These comparisons share outcomes and are not three independent dataset replications.

Recorded M5 sales are the target $Y$. After 56 uncensored warmup days, the fixed mechanical process gives
\begin{align}
 L_t&=0.90L_{t-1}+0.10S_{t-1},&
 C_t&=\max\{1,\lceil1.15L_t+0.25\sqrt{L_t+1}\rceil\},&
 S_t&=\min(Y_t,C_t).
 \label{eq:censor}
\end{align}
It hides 30.42\% of recorded demand mass and censors 12.67\% of development rows on days 1314--1913. Forecasts use origin-available information at horizons one through seven days. Retained complete targets train and calibrate the audit heads. Mechanical censoring creates heterogeneous errors in a controlled measurement setting; it is unnecessary for the mismatch theorem.

The UCI Bike Sharing test has 3,477 hourly observations from 147 held-out days. Disjoint, randomly assigned day groups fit the count predictor, fit its error head, calibrate selectors, and evaluate them. Features include recorded weather, so the task is conditional count prediction rather than forecasting future weather. The original predictors, five rankings, cap grid, and threshold rules were fixed before test outcomes were evaluated. The additional menu selection reuses that evidence (Appendix~\ref{app:nonretail}).

Two other M5 item partitions support the earlier experiments. The first holdout, named \emph{guardian} in saved files, has 610 items and shares development dates. Its initial 42-policy comparison was frozen; subsequent reuse is exploratory. The external partition has 610 different items and later forecast origins. Only its previously selected two-policy contrast received a one-time frozen test (Section~\ref{sec:external}). The additional menu and risk-screen analyses reuse previously evaluated data. Their chronological separation does not create a new confirmation trial.

\subsection{A common menu, two selection utilities}
\label{sec:choice-design}
The candidates rank conditional absolute error $\hat e$, row excess $\hat e-r\hat\mu$, exposure-normalized error, descending predicted exposure $-\hat\mu$, or mixed utility. The conditional-error comparator uses regression-based uncertainty estimation \citep{franc2023reject}, recalibrated to our ratio cap; it does not reproduce SELE training. For $U_\lambda=\lambda c+(1-\lambda)d$ and a nonbinding row floor, mixed utility orders
\begin{equation}
 s_\lambda=\frac{\hat e-r\hat\mu}{\lambda+(1-\lambda)\hat\mu/T},\qquad
 \lambda\in\{1,.25,0\},
 \label{eq:mixed}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is a fixed development estimate of mean exposure. Positive-exposure ordering at zero is equivalent to $\hat e/\hat\mu$. The additional menu uses no denominator floor and retains infeasible candidates as such.

Every candidate receives its largest feasible complete-tie threshold; all distinct scores are searched and accept-all is checked explicitly. M5 requires empirical risk at most $r$ and case coverage at least .35 in both A and B. Bike retains its original .95$r$ design margin and .35 case floor. The M5 reporting cap is $r=.6401298$, a 15\% reduction from the reference Poisson WAPE .7530939208 on the original point-selection rows; it is a study setting, not a retail standard. Bike's primary reporting cap is .85 times full calibration risk. The fixed sensitivity grids are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:policy-choice}.

Changing utility within one nested ranking cannot change its largest feasible threshold. Utility therefore selects between candidate rankings. Among eligible candidates $j$, we choose either
\begin{equation}
 j_C=\arg\max_j\min_{b\in\mathcal B}\widehat c_b(a_j),
 \qquad j_D=\arg\max_j\min_{b\in\mathcal B}\widehat d_b(a_j),
 \label{eq:menu-choice}
\end{equation}
with the same declared tie order. $\mathcal B$ is A/B for the retail menu and the calibration split for Bike. Test labels enter neither choice. An empty eligible menu returns no selected policy, with undefined accepted risk. This is a finite-menu audit, not global optimality over all gates.

\subsection{Risk screening and uncertainty}
A separate retail audit designs each of the 15 thresholds (three predictors, five rankings) on A at $0.95r$, then screens it on B at reporting cap $r$. For candidate $j$, item $i$ contributes the signed sum $G_{ij}=\sum_{t\in i}a_j(L_t-rW_t)$. A one-sided item-bootstrap upper bound on mean $G_{ij}$ must be nonpositive, with Bonferroni level $.05/15$ and positive accepted exposure. Among candidates that pass, equation~\eqref{eq:menu-choice} uses A utility only. A threshold is not adjusted to pass B. Later outcomes only evaluate the resulting selections.

All additional intervals use 4,000 paired cluster draws and condition on fits and selected policies. The eight primary case/exposure differences use 99.375\% intervals. Appendix~\ref{app:nonretail} preserves the original four-endpoint 98.75\% intervals; Table~\ref{tab:policy-choice} uses the expanded eight-endpoint family. Screen outcomes and pointwise 95\% risk intervals are separately identified. Item resampling retains stores and dates of each item; Bike resamples days. Shared shocks and dependence across clusters prevent distribution-free guarantees. The output is an approximate risk audit under stated dependence assumptions (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}), with all rejected candidates retained.

\section{Results}
\subsection{The coverage definition changes the selected rule}
\label{sec:policy-choice}
\input{policy_choice_table.tex}
Case coverage chooses the excess ranking for all four primary menus. Exposure coverage chooses descending predicted demand for all three retail predictors and the fitted ratio for Bike. Thus the practical implication is not to prescribe one score everywhere: the utility determines which candidate to select, and the empirical winner depends on the task.

These calibration choices retain their coverage contrast on evaluation data (Table~\ref{tab:policy-choice}). For the residual predictor, changing the selected rule raises covered demand from 83.85\% to 92.15\% while reducing accepted cases from 89.50\% to 54.32\%. All eight adjusted coverage intervals exclude zero. The three retail comparisons share items, and this additional analysis is retrospective. It measures the consequence of selecting among these five rules, not a general benchmark ranking of uncertainty estimators.

The cap sweep also changes the exposure-selected family: the fitted ratio is selected at stricter retail caps, whereas descending predicted exposure is selected at the original cap. At a sufficiently loose cap both choices accept all and the contrast vanishes. The conditional-error ranking has no feasible threshold at the primary caps under the stated floor. Every declared cap is retained in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:choice-caps}. At .68 only the residual predictor has zero contrast; L1 and Chronos retain it. Cap dependence is part of the result, not evidence for a universally preferred cap or score.

\subsection{The contrast survives a separate-window risk screen}
\label{sec:risk-screen}
Ten of 15 candidates have feasible A thresholds, and all ten pass the adjusted B screen. Thus B excludes no additional candidate: these results do not isolate a benefit of screening from the stricter A design. All three predictors retain different case- and exposure-selected rules. Later demand gains are 8.52, 11.76, and 9.04 points for L1, residual, and Chronos respectively. For the residual predictor, case-selected coverage falls from 89.50\% in the original menu to 75.36\%. Greater observed risk margin comes with lower acceptance.

All six selected policies have Later point risks between .5959 and .6039; their pointwise 95\% risk upper limits range from .6144 to .6257, below the reporting cap .6401 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:policy-screen}). These approximate, fit-conditional intervals are not simultaneous test guarantees or evidence of risk control under deployment shift. The new screen neither revises the original external protocol nor reverses its failed joint operating checks. It demonstrates a concrete selection procedure that keeps utility choice consequential after a separate risk check.

\subsection{Budget accounting explains the coverage difference}
\label{sec:mass}
For a common accepted set $K$ and disjoint additional set $U$, let $M_B$ and $E_B$ denote exposure and absolute-error mass. Additivity gives, for positive masses,
\begin{equation}
 R_{K\cup U}\leq r
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 M_U(R_U-r)\leq M_K(r-R_K).
 \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
The original retail direct-excess comparison gains 6.08 case points but loses 5.71 demand points relative to forecast-normalized error. Its added rows have near-unit WAPE and mean forecast .039, yet spend only 61.88\% of the common slack. The competing exclusive set carries roughly twice the demand while spending 52.01\% (Appendix~\ref{app:architecture}). The improved predictor retains the same low-exposure mechanism (Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy}).

Bike supplies a non-retail measurement without artificial censoring. Its row/ratio common set supplies 5,219 loss units of slack; the exclusive sets require 3,569 and 2,399. Each policy is point-feasible but the union is not. However, post-hoc day-bootstrap risk intervals for row, ratio, and union are respectively [.1203,.1409], [.1184,.1394], and [.1239,.1455], all crossing cap .1328. The coverage contrast is clear; population feasibility of each set and infeasibility of their union have not been jointly established. Union infeasibility alone would also not establish Pareto optimality. Appendix~\ref{app:policy-choice} gives the complete risk audit.

\subsection{What the one-time external test confirms}
\label{sec:external}\label{sec:heldout}
After inspecting the first item-holdout path, we replaced an earlier pending policy pair with $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=.25$, then froze their thresholds and first-seed heads before external access (Appendix~\ref{app:external}). The 610 unseen items supply 140,300 rows on days 1919--1941. All origins follow the last previously evaluated development day. Mixed utility gains 5.16 demand points and loses 3.35 case points (Table~\ref{tab:external}); both prespecified directional bounds exclude zero. This is confirmation of transfer of that selected contrast, not of the new menu procedure, the full utility path, or the population theorem.

Pooled point risks pass the cap, but all 24 adjusted risk upper bounds exceed it; all 24 case-coverage lower bounds pass. Hobbies and Household violate the cap even at their point estimates. In the saved-mask decomposition, each exclusive set spends about 30\% of the common slack, and their union remains point-feasible. The external result therefore establishes neither binding budget competition nor Pareto optimality. Its complete endpoint and access records remain unchanged.

\paragraph{Fitted-score and group limits.}\label{sec:objectives}\label{sec:groups}
Exact rankings need not order learned policies. Although $\partial(e/\mu)/\partial\mu=-e/\mu^2$, score amplification alone does not prove gate instability: for positive $\hat\mu$, $\hat e/\hat\mu\leq t$ is equivalent to $\hat e-t\hat\mu\leq0$. Boundary margins and joint estimation errors matter. A denominator floor changes the ranking; a positive row weight also changes the utility. The earlier floor sensitivity, architecture controls, and group interventions remain in Appendix~\ref{app:objective}. They do not establish a universally superior estimated ratio or resolve groupwise failure. Yeast shows no clear learned-policy reversal and Delicious is infeasible at its primary condition; both checks are retained (Appendix~\ref{app:nonretail}).

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{Choose utility before choosing a score.}
The exact-information ratio ranking does not prescribe the best estimated selector. At the primary retail cap, calibration favors descending predicted demand; at stricter caps it favors the fitted ratio. Bike also selects the ratio. These outcomes turn the theoretical distinction into an evaluation procedure: state what acceptance rewards, compare a common menu under the same cap, and report both coverages. The large case losses in Table~\ref{tab:policy-choice} are acceptable only if the stated exposure objective values that exchange. Reporting only the optimized coverage conceals this cost. Neither utility is universally preferable: equal service per case and service proportional to exposure answer different operational questions.

\paragraph{Improve prediction and acceptance separately.}
The residual predictor with mixed utility exceeds the original case-oriented pipeline in both coverages. This provides a constructive route for a forecasting system: improve its full-coverage predictions, then choose acceptance for the intended service objective. Risk verification remains a third task. Our stricter design preserves utility differences at lower risk, but the approximate pooled checks neither establish group guarantees nor measure inventory costs. Covered demand is a service measure, not realized economic value; deployment requires an explicitly specified cost objective and its own validation.

\section{Limitations}
The additional menu and split-screen analyses use previously evaluated data and a single fit per predictor. They are not new confirmation trials. Coverage intervals condition on that choice and fit; approximate cluster bootstrap does not remove shared store/calendar shocks or interday dependence. The five-score menu includes an error-regression comparator but not every learned selective predictor. The screened results concern pooled risk; the original groupwise and external joint checks remain unresolved. Bike uses a randomized day split and recorded weather, and the positive non-retail evidence still uses a WAPE-type risk. Natural-stockout identification is a separate limitation of the retail inputs (Appendix~\ref{app:natural-diagnostics}). The bounded-exposure envelope may be vacuous as its lower bound approaches zero, so it is not an informative retail error bar. Observed gaps do not estimate the worst-case bound, whose sharp construction permits exposure to approach zero. Forecasting and coverage gains are not measured profit or inventory-cost improvements.

\section{Conclusion}
More accepted predictions need not cover more exposure. Under a common ratio-risk cap, the two coverage objectives can disagree sharply because low-exposure cases consume little error budget. We quantify this disagreement and measure it through accepted-set accounting. The experiments show how specifying utility changes a finite-menu selection, and how better prediction can improve both coverages while leaving that choice consequential. The resulting recommendation is actionable: improve the predictor, select for the intended utility, and verify risk separately. Each step answers a different question; success at one does not establish the others.
